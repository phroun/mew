==== About Key Chords and Sequences ====

In music theory, a chord is more than one note played simultaneously.
In computing, a key chord is defined as pressing (and releasing) one
final key, while one or more other modifier keys are already being
held down.

This concept is fine for casual computing, but it suffers from three
distinct problems:

  1. There are only 26 letter keys, so only a few shortcuts can have an
     obvious mnemonic-device that helps facilitate their memory.

  2. Many basic functions require removing the hands far from the home
     row, which harms creative flow and efficiency.

  3. There is no obvious way to organize shortcuts into groups or to
     offer hints to the user, unless they resort to browsing drop-down
     menus or help manuals.

In 1978, Rob Barnaby's WordStar, a word processing application,
initially written for the CP/M operating system was published by
MicroPro International Corporation, and when it was later ported
to the IBM PC, it avoided these problems by leveraging **Key Sequences**
and using only a single Modifier Key, the Control key.

WordStar's **Key Sequences** were grouped into families, and each
family started with an initiator chord, such as Ctrl+K for "bloc__K__"
or Ctrl+Q for "__Q__uick", followed by one or more additional keys to
choose the specific command.  On-Screen Help was available, which could
teach the user both the top-level initiator chords, as well as the
potential completions available once the top-level initiator had been
pressed, and importantly, this help could be turned off by experts,
to reduce visual distraction while writing.

WordStar offered a "non-document" mode which became popular amongst
Computer Programmers as a "code editor", and many later text editors
and popular programmer's IDEs took inspiration from it.

Among these were Borland's Turbo Pascal IDE, made under the leadership
of Anders Hejlsberg, and the venerable JOE (Joe's Own Editor), originally
written and released by Joseph Allen in 1991.

**mew** also makes use of **Chords** and **Key Sequences**, and its default
key mappings are organized into command families in much the same spirit
as WordStar and JOE.
